# Storytelling

Jobs's keynotes made people believe in a future before they could hold it. Imitators copied the turtleneck and the timeline, and their stories collapsed because the product did not do what the story said. So everything here serves a real thing, shown working.

## Pick the shape

| Piece | Shape |
|---|---|
| Launch, pitch, announcement, talk, blog post, demo | The full arc |
| Design doc, PR body, status update, README | Answer first, then borrow: timeline framing, the problem as villain, numbers in context, show it working |
| Short message | voice-style alone |

## The arc

1. **What is.** Open on the reader's world today, in their words, with the pain made concrete. iPhone, 2007: smartphones are "not so smart and not so easy to use", plotted on two axes.
2. **Villain.** Name what stands in the way: an approach, a habit, a tool. "Who wants a stylus?" Aim at the approach and keep respect for the people using it.
3. **Timeline.** Place the new thing as the next step of a progression the reader already accepts: mouse, click wheel, multi-touch. Build it from real history; an invented progression costs the writer their credibility once it fails.
4. **Reveal.** The headline: one sentence a reader could repeat to a friend. Build toward it, as with "an iPod, a phone, and an internet communicator" turning out to be one device.
5. **Show it working.** Prove the reveal right after it lands, with output from really running the thing: a screenshot, a clip, a command and what it printed. Each later claim sits next to its own proof or a measured number.
6. **What could be.** The reader's life with it, from their seat. Swing between what is and what could be until the gap is obvious.
7. **One more thing.** Save one true surprise for the end, or call back to the opening line. The last line is the strongest one.

## Sentence techniques

- **Analogy.** One familiar image carries the whole idea: "a bicycle for the mind". At most one per piece, returned to, and true when pressed.
- **Concrete.** The felt experience in place of the spec: "1,000 songs in your pocket" for 5 GB, "you can throw it out the window" for 12 pounds.
- **Numbers in context.** Pair each number with a comparison the reader already holds: "1% of the market is 10 million phones".
- **Rapport.** Stand beside the reader, looking at the thing together: "you", "let's look", a real reaction ("you had me at scrolling").
- **Redefinition.** Reframe a word or category with a fact: Apple in 1997 as "the largest education company in the world".
- **Soul.** For a personal piece: a few true stories, each built from concrete details (an age, a place, an object), a turn, and a one-line lesson, with one refrain and a closing callback. Stanford, 2005: "three stories... no big deal".
- **Showmanship.** At most one staged beat per piece (a misdirection, a prop, a pause on the hero moment), spent on something true.

## Demos

A demo is the arc in miniature: the pain in one line, the hero action, the result on screen, the number. One idea per demo, the hero moment early, and real data. In a video or live demo, every narrated noun is on screen as it is said; in a written demo, each step shows its command or screenshot beside the sentence about it.

## Check

The full arc checks every item; a work doc checks the proof, number, and timeline items.

- [ ] The headline fits in one repeatable sentence.
- [ ] The villain is an approach, named.
- [ ] Each claim sits next to its demo or measured number.
- [ ] Each number has a comparison.
- [ ] The timeline is real history.
- [ ] One analogy and one showmanship beat at most.
- [ ] The last line is a callback or the strongest line.
